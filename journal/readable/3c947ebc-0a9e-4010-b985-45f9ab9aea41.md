## Lesson 1: Hodgkin–Huxley and the extracellular spike

**Why it matters:** each neuron's waveform shape is what the sorter uses to tell neurons apart, so the shape needs a physical basis.

**The model.** The membrane acts like a capacitor with three parallel current paths:

$$C\frac{dV}{dt} = I_{ext} - \bar g_{Na}\,m^3h\,(V-E_{Na}) - \bar g_K\,n^4\,(V-E_K) - g_L\,(V-E_L)$$

- **m** is the sodium activation gate. It is fast and opens when the cell depolarizes.
- **h** is the sodium inactivation gate. It is slow and closes when the cell depolarizes, which ends the sodium current.
- **n** is the potassium activation gate. It is slow and opens to bring V back down, which repolarizes the cell.

Each gate follows $\frac{dx}{dt} = \alpha_x(V)(1-x) - \beta_x(V)\,x$. Here α is the voltage-dependent rate at which closed gates open, and β is the rate at which open gates close. Together you get 4 coupled ODEs (V, m, h, n), and you'll integrate them with RK4.

**From inside the cell to the electrode.** The electrode doesn't measure V. It measures the potential that membrane current creates in the fluid around the cell. For a point source, $\phi \approx \frac{I_m}{4\pi\sigma r}$, where σ is the conductivity of the tissue fluid. During the upstroke, positive current flows *into* the cell, so the extracellular spike is **negative first**. Its shape looks roughly like −dV/dt or the summed ionic current, not like V.

**A subtlety we need to decide on (it may come up on Dec 3).** In a single-compartment model with no injected current, total membrane current sums to zero. Real extracellular spikes exist because current flows *in* at one part of the cell and *out* at another, which forms a dipole. A dipole falls off as **1/r²**, not 1/r. You have two options:
- Use the ionic current as the waveform shape and assume 1/r scaling. This is simple and common.
- Model it as a dipole with 1/r². This is more realistic.

Neither is wrong, but you'll need to state and justify whichever you pick.

**Claude:** # Summary: Quiroga, Nadasdy & Ben-Shaul (2004)

**The problem.** Sorting spikes by hand is slow and biased. Automatic methods usually rely on features like PCA and on clustering methods that assume Gaussian clusters, and both assumptions can fail. The authors build a pipeline that runs with no human input and test it on simulated data, where the correct answers are known. That is the same strategy as your project.

**The pipeline:**
1. **Detection.**
   - They bandpass filter at **300–6000 Hz** with a 4-pole Butterworth filter.
   - The threshold is 4σₙ, with σₙ = median(|x|)/0.6745. Their Fig. 3 is your "robust σ vs. plain std" check: as firing rate rises, plain std overestimates the noise, while the median estimate stays close to the true value.
   - They save **64 samples** per spike (about 2.5 ms) and align each spike so its peak sits at sample 20. The peak is found on a cubic-spline upsampled version of the waveform.
2. **Features.**
   - They apply a 4-level Haar transform, which gives 64 coefficients per spike.
   - They keep the **10 coefficients** whose distributions are least normal, measured with the Lilliefors version of the KS test.
   - The reasoning: a coefficient that separates neurons should have several peaks in its distribution, and a distribution with several peaks isn't Gaussian.
   - Before testing, they keep only values within ±3 SD, so overlapping spikes don't make a coefficient look non-normal.
   - In their Fig. 7, picking coefficients by highest variance instead gives flat, single-peaked distributions that don't separate anything.
3. **Clustering: superparamagnetic clustering (SPC).**
   - Each spike's neighbors in feature space tend to change state together at a given "temperature."
   - At low temperature everything forms one cluster. At high temperature it breaks into dust. In between, the real clusters appear.
   - They choose the temperature automatically: the highest one at which a new cluster with more than 60 spikes appears.
   - SPC makes no Gaussian assumption.

**How they simulated data.**
- Spike shapes come from a database of 594 *real* recorded spikes, with each peak scaled to 1.
- **Background noise is made of many randomly placed small spikes from that database.** That gives the noise the same spectrum as the spikes, which makes sorting harder than white noise would.
- Three neurons fire as Poisson processes at 20 Hz with a 2 ms refractory period.
- Noise SD is 0.05–0.2 of the spike peak (up to 0.4 in one example).
- They simulate at 96 kHz and then downsample to 24 kHz, so spike peaks don't line up exactly with samples.

**Results:**
- **Detection (Table 1):** they miss few spikes until noise reaches 0.2.
  - One neuron had a positive-first spike, and the positive-only threshold missed many of its spikes.
  - When noise is very low, a spike's second peak can cross threshold and get counted as a second spike (a false positive).
- **Sorting (Table 2):**
  - With SPC, the average error count was 232 for wavelets, 1092 for PCA, and 873 for the raw waveform.
  - **With K-means, the gap almost disappeared: 332 vs. 371.** In a few cases PCA did better, because wavelet clusters were stretched out in a way K-means can't handle.
  - The number of clusters found drops as noise rises.
- **Harder conditions:** SPC + wavelets still found all three neurons in three tests: an electrode drifting (amplitude falling from 1 to 0.3), a bursting neuron (spike amplitudes 1.0, 0.7, 0.5 about 3 ms apart), and spike amplitude tied to the background signal.
- Most errors came from overlapping spikes less than about 0.5 ms apart.

## Walkthrough of [hh.py](spikesort/hh.py)

**Lines 1–22: units and parameters.** These are the classic HH values. E_L = −54.387 is chosen so that the leak current exactly cancels the small Na and K currents at −65 mV. That's what makes −65 mV the resting potential. *This is the squid axon at 6.3 °C with no temperature correction yet*, as your deviation #4 says. The ~1 ms width check belongs to the waveform step, which comes next.

**Lines 25–34: `_x_over_1_minus_exp`.** α_m and α_n have the form x / (1 − e^(−x/k)). At V = −40 mV (for m) or −55 mV (for n), x = 0 and this becomes 0/0. Mathematically the limit is k, so the function returns k when x is essentially 0. Without this, a trace that passed exactly through −40.000 mV would crash.

**Lines 37–45: `rates(v)`.** These are the six standard HH rate functions in 1/ms. α is how fast closed gates open and β is how fast open gates close. Notice that α_m grows steeply with depolarization, which is the start of your positive feedback loop. α_h *falls* with depolarization while β_h rises, so h closes.

**Lines 48–54: `steady_state(v)`.** If V were held fixed, each gate would settle where opening balances closing: α(1 − x) = βx, so x∞ = α / (α + β). We start the simulation from these values at −65 mV, so the cell begins truly at rest. That's why the rest test drifts only 0.007 mV.

**Lines 57–63: `ionic_currents`.** These are the three conductance × driving force terms. Two things to notice:
- **m³h** and **n⁴** model one channel as several independent gates that must all be open at once. Sodium needs three m gates open and the h gate open; potassium needs four n gates open.
- The driving force (V − E_Na) is why the peak can't pass +50 mV, which ties back to your Q4 answer. This function is separate because **the extracellular waveform will be built from these currents** next.

**Lines 66–76: `hh_rhs`.** This is the whole model: four equations giving dV/dt, dm/dt, dh/dt and dn/dt. The V line is C·dV/dt = I_ext − I_Na − I_K − I_L. The three gate lines all follow the same rule: dx/dt = α(1 − x) − βx. It takes `t` as an input because the injected current can change over time.

**Lines 79–90: the integrators.**
- `euler_step` uses one slope, measured at the start of the step.
- `rk4_step` measures four slopes: at the start (k1), twice at the midpoint (k2 uses k1 to get there, k3 uses k2), and at the end (k4 uses k3). It then averages them with weights 1:2:2:1. That averaging cancels error terms up to dt⁴.
- Both are generic: they take any `f(t, y)`, so nothing in them is specific to HH.

**Lines 93–101: input currents.** `pulse` returns a *function*: you set the pulse up once, and the integrator can then ask for the current at any time t, including the half-steps RK4 uses.

**Lines 104–121: `simulate`.** It picks the step method, builds the time grid, and stores every state in an (n_steps + 1) × 4 array (column 0 is V). It then loops, each new state computed from the previous one. The inner `f` "bakes in" the chosen current, so the step functions only ever see `f(t, y)`.

**Lines 124–127: `count_spikes`.** It marks each sample as above or below 0 mV and counts the places where one sample is below and the next is above (`~above[:-1] & above[1:]`). A spike is one upward crossing, however long it stays above.

**Claude:** That's not quite it. k2 and k3 aren't more accurate than k1 and k4. Each is just the slope at a different *place*. The weights come from what the step is actually doing.

**What the step computes:** to move from y(t) to y(t + dt), you need the area under the slope curve over the step:

$$y(t+dt) = y(t) + \int_t^{t+dt} \text{slope}\;ds$$

**Simpson's rule** estimates that area from the slope at the start, middle and end:

$$\int \approx \frac{dt}{6}\big(\text{start} + 4\cdot\text{middle} + \text{end}\big)$$

It fits a parabola through those three points, and for a parabola the area comes out with exactly these weights. The middle point gets a weight of 4 because it sits closest to all the area, not because its value is more trustworthy.

**RK4 is Simpson's rule with two estimates of the middle slope**, k2 and k3. Neither is exact, so RK4 splits the 4 between them: 1 + **2 + 2** + 1, over 6. Averaging the two midpoint estimates cancels some of each one's error, and that's where RK4's 4th order comes from.

**Claude:** `pytest.ini` is a small settings file that pytest reads automatically whenever you run `pytest` from the project folder. Yours has two settings:

```ini
[pytest]
testpaths = tests
pythonpath = .
```

- **`testpaths = tests`** tells pytest to look for tests only in the `tests/` folder. Without it, pytest would search the whole project, including `.venv/` with all of numpy's files, which is slower and can turn up tests that aren't yours.
- **`pythonpath = .`** adds the project root (`.` means "this folder") to the list of places Python looks for imports. Your test files start with `from spikesort import hh`, but they live in `tests/`, and by default Python wouldn't look one level up to find `spikesort/`. You saw this happen: the very first test run failed with `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'spikesort'`, and adding this line fixed it.

It also does a third thing just by being there: pytest treats the folder containing `pytest.ini` as the project's **root directory**. That's the `rootdir:` and `configfile: pytest.ini` lines at the top of your test output.

You commit it with your code, so anyone who clones your repo can run `pytest` and get the same setup.
